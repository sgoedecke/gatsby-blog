

## Thinking notes

Work is definitely role-playing, and of course it's possible to damage yourself by playing a bad role, or losing yourself in a role (having it become a habit).

Is distance between your personal self and your work self good or bad? Good, because it protects your secret personal life from the push and pull of work; bad because any time spent not being your true authentic self risks losing it. But it's unclear that you can be your true authentic self at work, doing wage labor for pay, etc - if you think you shouldn't ever play a role, don't you have to refuse roles entirely? in vaneigem's terms:

> Those whom Power can neither govern nor kill, it taxes with madness

Talking about the role you're playing is good. It introduces deliberate playfulness, irony, etc - all good antidotes to taking yourself too seriously.

Contorting yourself to live out some kind of social role _sounds_ bad, but is it? What's the alternative? Real, authentic living a la the cult in Impro, or going off to see a guru, or whatever? Isn't that just playing a different role (the role of the authentic iconoclast?) It's unclear to me that humans can meaningfully exist in the absence of any kind of social structure; it's just such a core part of what we are (see Wittgenstein on private language).

Can't be too general here - it's just miles out of my wheelhouse. I'm writing about _my_ job and _my_ situation, not about the relationship of labour to work or anything like that.

"I am a craftsman at my job" strikes me as more role-playing than "I am a staff engineer looking to produce shareholder value": for one, it's just less true (you are _labor_); for two, it's explicitly adopting the role of the 2010s that tech companies tried to sell their employees on (to extract harder, more conscientious work, to attract new hires, etc). But I suppose "fuck the bosses, I'm in it for the craft and if I get fired I get fired" is more authentic still

individuality vs fungibility - making yourself fungible is part of being professional, but it is dehumanizing.

a lot of the writing on this kind of implies that you can't ever take off the mask - that you do your job then come home to your Regularly Scheduled Normal Entertaiment, then repeat, then go mad. Kind of a Fight Club vibe here. But I don't know... you just don't have to do that?

## Marx: estranged labour

https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1844/epm/1st.htm#s4

marx builds alienation out of his theory of proletarian misery: the more he produces, the poorer he is; the more beautiful his product, the more deformed he is; etc.

> the more the worker produces, the less he has to consume;
the more value he creates, the more worthless he becomes;
the more his product is shaped, the more misshapen the worker;
the more civilized his object, the more barbarous the worker;
the more powerful the work, the more powerless the worker;
the more intelligent the work, the duller the worker and the
more he becomes a slave of nature.

I don't think this naturally applies to big tech at all, where productive engineers are typically well-compensated and healthy. I think Marx is thinking of a factory, where a craftsman who can make (say) really good chairs is reduced to performing a series of rigid actions like a machine - is knowledge work like this? I don't think engineers are a cheap commodity at all.

however, it's true that the power of my labor is independent of me: the more I invest in my work, the more I empower my employer, not me, and since my employer is fundamentally in an adversarial relationship with me (in some sense), my work is contrary to my own interests. But that "in some sense" is doing far too much work here - for SWEs, a company doing badly is awful; a company doing well kind of lifts all boats.

> the workers is related to the product of labor as to an alien object. For it is clear that, according to this premise, the more the worker exerts himself in his work, the more powerful the alien, objective world becomes which he brings into being over against himself, the poorer he and his inner world become, and the less they belong to him.

A lot of this is dependent on the "the more I produce the poorer I am" thesis - if this is true, the worker's production is truly an alien force, as if he's working to create an alien object that is opposed to him. But does it hold up without that?

> Firstly, the fact that labor is external to the worker -- i.e., does not belong to his essential being; that he, therefore, does not confirm himself in his work, but denies himself, feels miserable and not happy, does not develop free mental and physical energy, but mortifies his flesh and ruins his mind. Hence, the worker feels himself only when he is not working; when he is working, he does not feel himself. He is at home when he is not working, and not at home when he is working. His labor is, therefore, not voluntary but forced, it is forced labor. It is, therefore, not the satisfaction of a need but a mere means to satisfy needs outside itself. Its alien character is clearly demonstrated by the fact that as soon as no physical or other compulsion exists, it is shunned like the plague. External labor, labor in which man alienates himself, is a labor of self-sacrifice, of mortification. Finally, the external character of labor for the worker is demonstrated by the fact that it belongs not to him but to another, and that in it he belongs not to himself but to another. Just as in religion the spontaneous activity of the human imagination, the human brain, and the human heart, detaches itself from the individual and reappears as the alien activity of a god or of a devil, so the activity of the worker is not his own spontaneous activity. It belongs to another, it is a loss of his self.

Interesting - he does not "confirm himself in his work". That part I agree with.

Kind of three versions here: the factory-worker "the more i work the poorer I get" version, the more theoretical "the more I work the more i empower my employer relative to myself" version, and the purely mental "the more I work, the more I follow someone else's ends, the more I lose myself" colloquial version.

## Vaneigem: the revolution of everyday life

https://situationist.org/book/revolution-of-everyday-life/chapter/chapter-15-roles

> The roles we play in everyday life, on the other hand, soak into the individual, preventing him from being what he really is and what he really wants to be. They are nuclei of alienation embedded in the flesh of direct experience. The function of such stereotypes is to dictate to each person on an individual, even 'intimate', level the same things which ideology imposes collectively.

> hybrid parts, parts which appear to answer our desires, but which are really antagonistic to them

this sounds like Marx

> At the opposite extreme from absolute identification is a particular way of putting a distance between the role and one's self, a way of establishing a zone of free play. This zone is a breeding place of attitudes disruptive of the spectacular order. Nobody is ever completely swallowed up by a role. Even turned on its head, the will to live retains a potential for violence always capable of carrying the individual away from the path laid down for him. One fine morning, the faithful lackey, who has hitherto identified completely with his master, leaps on his oppressor and slits his throat.

> . Life is sacrificed, and the loss compensated by means of accomplished prestidigitation in the realm of appearances. The more daily life is thus impoverished, the greater the attraction of inauthenticity, and vice versa. Dislodged from its essential place by the bombardment of prohibitions, limitations and lies, lived reality comes to seem so trivial that appearances become the centre of our attention, until roles completely obscure the importance of our own lives. In an order of things, compensation is the only thing that gives a person any weight. The role compensates for a lack: ultimately, for the lack of life; more immediately, for the lack of another role. A worker conceals his prostration beneath the role of foreman, and the poverty of this role itself beneath the incomparably superior image of a late-model car. But every role is paid for by self-injury (overwork, the renunciation of 'luxuries', survival, etc.). ... This ambiguity accounts to my mind for people's addiction to roles. It explains why roles stick to our skin, why we give up our lives for them. They impoverish real experience but they also protect this experience from becoming conscious of its impoverishment. Indeed, so brutal a revelation would probably be too much for an isolated individual to take. Thus roles partake of organized isolation, of separation, of false union, while compensation is the depressant that ensures the realization of all the potentialities of inauthenticity, that gets us high on identification.

## Debord: society of the spectacle

https://situationist.org/book/sots/chapter/chapter-1-separation-perfected

> The alienation of the spectator to the profit of the contemplated object (which is the result of his own unconscious activity) is expressed in the following way: the more he contemplates the less he lives; the more he accepts recognizing himself in the dominant images of need, the less he understands his own existence and his own desires. The externality of the spectacle in relation to the active man appears in the fact that his own gestures are no longer his but those of another who represents them to him. This is why the spectator feels at home nowhere, because the spectacle is everywhere.

> The worker does not produce himself; he produces an independent power. The success of this production, its abundance, returns to the producer as an abundance of dispossession. All the time and space of his world become foreign to him with the accumulation of his alienated products. The spectacle is the map of this new world, a map which exactly covers its territory. The very powers which escaped us show themselves to us in all their force.

good articulation - it's dehumanizing because they're not your gestures; it's alienating because (as Marx said) the worker is producing an independent power that rises up against him (in what sense, exactly?)

## Goffman: the presentation of the self in everyday life

## Mills: WHite Collar

https://history.hanover.edu/courses/excerpts/234mills-collar.html

> {21} In the case of the white-collar man, the alienation of the wage-worker from the products of his work is carried one step nearer to its Kafka-like completion. The salaried employee does not make anything, although he may handle much that he greatly desires but cannot have. No product of craftsmanship can be his to contemplate with pleasure as it is being created and after it is made. Being alienated from any product of his labor, and going year after year through the same paper routine, he turns his leisure all the more frenziedly to the ersatz diversion that is sold him, and partakes of the synthetic excitement that neither eases nor releases. He is bored at work and restless at play, and this terrible alternation wears him out.

> {22} In his work he often clashes with customer and superior, and must almost always be the standardized loser: he must smile and be personable, standing behind the counter, or waiting in the outer office. In many strata of white-collar employment, such traits as courtesy, helpfulness, and kindness, once intimate, are now part of the impersonal means of livelihood. Self-alienation is thus an accompaniment of his alienated labor.

> {23} When white-collar people get jobs, they sell not only their time and energy but their personalities as well. They sell by the week or month their smiles and their kindly gestures, and they must practice the prompt repression of resentment and aggression. For these intimate traits are of commercial relevance and required for the more efficient and profitable distribution of goods and services. Here are the new little Machiavellians, practicing their personable crafts for hire and for the profit of others, according to rules laid down by those above them.

The prompt repression of resentment - yes! "No product of craftsmanship is his to contemplate with pleasure as it is made" - this is a kind of quite literal alienation, where the physical separation of maker and made thing is the "alienation" in question.

## Hochschild: emotion work, feeling rules and social structure

Feeling rules - rules about when it's appropriate to have certain feelings and when not. In a sense this is always kind of inauthentic - the true authentic human would simply feel what they feel - but it's unavoidable to have some norms around this.

> When I speak of social class, it is not strictly speaking to income, education, or occupational status that I refer, but to something roughly correlated to these-the on-the-job task of creating and sustaining appropriate meanings. The bank manager, the IBM executive, for example, may be required, in part, to sustain a definition of self, office, and organization as "up and coming," or "on the go," "caring," or "reliable," meanings most effectively sustained through acts upon feeling. Feeling rules are of utmost salience in jobs such as these; rule reminders and sanctions are more in play. 

some working-class jobs (e.g. truck driver) have less; others (e.g. waitress) have more

in general, "deep acting" for Hochschild means "method acting" - actually eliciting the relevant feelings. So if you spend your workday "deep acting", it can be alienating - but this is worse for waiters and secretaries, and not so bad for SWEs. Worse for staff+, I think, but still not that bad.
