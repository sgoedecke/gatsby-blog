That's a tough situation, and a very common one. It's easy for a manager to tell someone they need to lead more projects, but it's hard for a manager to actually hand someone projects to lead. It's always going to be a safer choice to give new work to someone who's proven and reliable.

How you should approach this depends on why you're not being chosen. If it's simply habit, you could directly remind your manager you're looking to lead projects. If it's a lack of trust, then you'll need to start slower and try and demonstrate that you're capable and reliable (typically by doing a good job as a non-lead). Could you suggest candidate low-stakes projects to your manager?

If it's a deliberate political move to hoard credit, that's harder: there's likely very little you can do aside from trying to build a good relationship with your skip and boosting the visibility of the work you do get handed. In the worst case, you'll have to figure out work you can do by yourself - in the blog I call these "side bets" - though that's risky.

Sometimes responsibility is taken, not given. If you see a problem, fix a problem - that sort of thing. It depends on how your team is structured and how process-bound you are. I've worked on teams where that kind of thing is rewarded and on teams where it's punished.
