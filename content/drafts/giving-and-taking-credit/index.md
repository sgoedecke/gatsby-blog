Engineers often complain that visibility should be their manager's job. In other words, they should be able to focus on the code, while their company figures out which engineers are strong and rewards them.

This attitude is an extension of the "school fantasy": the idea that people who excelled at the standardized tests and structure of school should be able to step into a company that works the same way. After all, you didn't have to worry about "visibility" at school or university. You simply did the assignments and tests you were given, and if you did well you were rewarded with a good grade.

Many big tech companies encourage this attitude, because it helps them recruit smart graduates. They fashion their workplaces to look and feel like a university, even calling them "campuses". But it's still work, not school. If you treat it like school, you are going to have a bad time.

### Taking credit

The first lesson many new engineers learn is that **you have to take credit for your work**. If you silently jump in to help a struggling project and get it back on track, there's no guarantee of reward. Credit will naturally flow to the project lead. In fact, if this project is outside of your direct team, it's likely you will be punished for it: to your manager, it will look like you're simply doing nothing at all.

Even when your manager is watching your work, **credit is largely uncorrelated with how well you did**. Quickly solving a hard technical problem is often met with a "ok, what's next?" response. Other times, the most trivial fixes are celebrated as if you'd won a Nobel.

The reason is that, unlike at school, **you are the subject-matter expert on your own work**. Software systems are so complicated that [only the people who work on them](/you-cant-design-software-you-dont-work-on/) can hope to understand them, and even that understanding is always[ imperfect](/in-defense-of-not-understanding-your-codebase/). If even experts can't reliably [estimate](/how-i-estimate-work/) the difficulty of changes, how is your manager supposed to assess your technical performance?

The answer is they don't. There's no professor of your specific codebase hovering in the wings, grading papers. Instead, smart managers will find engineers on your team they trust and ask them how you're doing. On small teams that have worked on a single codebase for a long time, this works okay, because everyone's familiar enough to judge everyone else's work. On large teams with a high rate of codebase churn, it goes badly, since they're just guessing. On teams with a nasty, cutthroat culture, it sometimes goes _very_ badly, since this is a good opportunity to actively sabotage the engineers who might threaten you.

The only solution is to **take the credit yourself**. When you do something good, tell your manager that you did it. Write an internal post explaining why it was technically difficult and how you solved it. Actively [build trust](/point-person/) with your management chain. In other words, you have to start [playing politics](/playing-politics/).

### Giving credit

There's a kind of engineer who's learned this lesson but hasn't learned any other lessons yet. They're proactive about telling people what they've done, and they always maintain a ["brag doc"](https://jvns.ca/blog/brag-documents/). You can tell they're jealously guarding whatever credit they've managed to accumulate. In particular, they love to talk about the parts they did _by themselves_, since those are least vulnerable to other people coming in to claim credit.

The lesson this kind of engineer hasn't learned is that **you can often accumulate credit best by giving it away**. To see why, consider how credit flows _up_ inside a tech company.

I wrote above that your manager can't assess the quality of your technical work on their own, but instead has to rely on other engineers they trust who they can quietly ask "hey, was this project really that impressive?". In fact, your manager doesn't decide who gets promoted or who gets a raise. They only make recommendations to their manager. That manager has their own perspective, and their own network of trusted engineers who they can quietly ask "hey, was this project really that impressive?". Sometimes this even goes up another layer or two[^1]. The point is that **there is a large group of people behind the scenes who will quietly judge the value of your work**.

A shared project you've worked on reflects well on everybody: on you, for working well with others, on the people you've worked with, for the same reason, and for your manager, for fostering such a great environment of cooperation. Lots of people have good reason to talk that project up. On the other hand, a project you've jealously kept to yourself reflects well on nobody: you come across as antisocial and your peers come across as unhelpful.
