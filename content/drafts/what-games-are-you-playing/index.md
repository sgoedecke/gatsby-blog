In grad school, I tried really hard to get good at analytic philosophy. I spent a lot of time and effort learning how to operate the conceptual machinery involved. In my experience, this was largely about learning to chop a seemingly-obvious statement into a series of impossibly-fine distinctions, perform some logical operation, then work your way back up to demonstrate what that means with respect to the original statement. I was [pretty good](https://github.com/sgoedecke/gatsby-blog/blob/master/content/philosophy/intuitionist-moral-perception.pdf) at it, though I had peers in my program who were better.

At various times in my life, I've tried to get good at the video game [Dota 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dota_2). This was largely about gaining an intuitive sense of what your top two priorities are at any given time (out of a total list of hundreds, shifting constantly) and being in the right place to fulfil them. I only ever managed to be _fine_ at the game: in the top 15-20% of players[^1], but I would routinely run into players who would destroy me.

I have spent the last ten years of my career trying to get good at [shipping projects in big tech companies](/how-to-ship/). This is largely about working in [large codebases](/large-established-codebases/), doing [system](/good-system-design/) and [API](/good-api-design/) design, and [not making enemies](/playing-politics/). I'd say I'm pretty good at this: I'm very happy with the state of my career, particularly given I'm working fully remote from Australia, and I've made the jump to staff engineer [twice](/staff-engineer-promotions/).

I have spent the last two years trying to get good at [blogging to a technical audience](/on-writing/). This is largely about writing well, trying hard to articulate the most obvious-yet-controversial ideas I have, and developing a thick skin to online criticism[^2]. There are plenty of better bloggers out there, but I was the [third most popular](/2025-wrapup/) personal blog on Hacker News in 2025, and I'm happy with the amount of high-quality reader engagement I get (mostly via email).

These are all examples of games. Some of them are for very low stakes, like Dota 2. Some of them are for very high stakes: how good I am at working in tech companies will determine the shape of my career, and thus in large part the shape of my life. But they all have the same basic structure:

- Success is determined by proficiency with a set of skills
- It is not obvious what that set of skills is at the outset, or how to improve at it
- In the short term, it isn't obvious whether you're improving (though it should be obvious in the medium-to-long term whether you're succeeding)

Skill at one game doesn't usually transfer to skill at another game. But skill at _learning a game_ does, because games share the same structure. This explains the unfair reality that people who are good at one thing tend to be good at other things too. Life isn't like D&D character creation where expertise in one area means being awful in others. Students who are good at sports also tend to get [good grades](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/middle-school-jocks-actually-get-the-best-grades-155005781/).

People who are good at several different things tend to be people who are good at deliberately figuring out what skills they need to succeed, and then practicing those skills even when they're not sure they're making progress[^3]. People who are not very good at things often don't even know that deliberate practice is an option. Sometimes they've done it for one game (for instance, with the help of a coach), but haven't learned how to do it in general.

The alternative to deliberate practice is aimless practice: just doing the task in front of you, without thinking too hard about the meta-skills involved. You will get a little better over time that way, but you'll get absolutely smoked by anyone taking it more seriously. For much more on this, I recommend David Sirlin's book [_Playing to Win_](https://www.sirlin.net/ptw).

The bigger problem with aimless practice is that **you can go for years without even knowing what game you're playing**
